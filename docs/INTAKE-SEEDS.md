# Fox & Found — Intake Seeds

Raw material for authoring the ~50 fox cases. These are **seeds, not stories**:
each one is a real, documented reason foxes arrive at wildlife rescues, with a
note on how such cases typically resolve. Pick a seed, then build the case
with `docs/CASE-TEMPLATE.md` — design purpose first, welfare razor always.

Researched 2026-10-04 from rescue case logs, rehab association publications,
and veterinary/wildlife-medicine literature (full report and sources in the
research notes). Figures below are index-level, not live-verified; outcome
splits are inferred from case reports rather than published tables.

## Outcome context (for realism)

- The Fox Project (London) rescues ~1,400 foxes/year; roughly half survive to release.
- A 600,000-record North American dataset found vehicle collisions the top cause, ~40% of intakes human-caused, ~1/3 of animals released.
- A mammal rehab study found mortality highest for gunshot/poisoning (~100%) and vehicle collision (93%), lowest for captivity issues (15%) — but captivity cases are almost never releasable.

## 1. Vehicle strikes and other trauma

1. **RTA — fractured limb.** The single most common adult-fox admission. Repairable fractures (splints/external fixators) are routinely released; compound or comminuted fractures often end in euthanasia.
2. **RTA — head injury/concussion.** Fox found dazed or circling at the roadside; mild concussions recover with rest and release, severe brain injury is euthanized.
3. **RTA — spinal trauma, hind-leg paralysis.** Long-shot rehab: months of cage rest and hydrotherapy; may end in permanent sanctuary placement if mobility never returns.
4. **RTA — pelvic fracture.** Common in vehicle strikes; guarded prognosis, cage rest is the mainstay. Often arrives with concurrent issues (e.g. mange).
5. **Leg-hold trap injury.** Crushed toes, degloved feet, partial amputations from foothold traps; severe cases euthanized, adapted animals placed permanently.
6. **Snare around the neck.** Deep lacerations and infection risk; animals found early and treated can be released after observation.
7. **Snare on a limb.** Circulation loss, tendon damage, sometimes self-amputation; early finds recover and release, necrotic limbs mean euthanasia.
8. **Entanglement in football/garden netting.** A top urban intake reason — foxes thrash in soccer nets, arrive with infected abrasions; most released after wound care and antibiotics.
9. **Fence impalement.** Foxes impale themselves on barbed wire or chain-link; classic urban/rural-edge admission, released after weeks of care if no organ damage.
10. **Paw wedged in fence.** Brief dramatic rescues — fox hanging by a trapped paw; usually immediate release once the limb checks out.
11. **Dog attack.** Among the most common admissions at busy hospitals; puncture wounds and crush injuries, cubs especially vulnerable. Clean wounds release; severe cases euthanized.
12. **Cat attack on a cub.** Small-carnivore attacks are a documented cub intake route; treatable with antibiotics and wound care.
13. **Airgun/pellet-gun shooting.** X-rays reveal pellets; severe cases euthanized with evidence passed to police (cruelty investigations follow). Survivable wounds treat and release.
14. **Arrow/crossbow bolt injury.** Bolts through neck or shoulder; surgical removal and months of recovery, usually releasable. Some rescues see roughly a dozen arrow cases a year.
15. **Cub trapped in a construction pit.** Cubs found exhausted and caked in mud at building sites — a recurring construction hazard; bathed, fed, and released.
16. **Cubs wedged between buildings.** Cubs stuck in narrow gaps; sometimes need firefighter extraction and oxygen; released together with support feeding.

## 2. Illness and medical conditions

17. **Sarcoptic mange.** The signature fox disease — crusting, hair loss, starvation. Highly treatable (ivermectin or single-dose fluralaner); most treated foxes are released.
18. **Demodectic mange.** Less common, usually in immunocompromised or very young animals; patchy hair loss rather than thick crusting. Releasable once resolved — and "mange" alone is not a diagnosis.
19. **Canine distemper.** Respiratory signs progressing to neurological damage; no cure. Rehab centers generally euthanize symptomatic foxes to prevent spread.
20. **Rabies-suspicion protocol.** Abnormal behavior (loss of fear, aggression, paralysis) triggers isolation-or-euthanasia protocols. These cases essentially never end in release.
21. **Intestinal parasites.** Roundworms and coccidia routine in cubs; standard deworming is part of nearly every kit intake. Uncomplicated cases release.
22. **French heartworm.** Lung-vessel parasite (common in European foxes, rare in North America); treatable with dewormers over weeks. Illustrates parasite treatment in a held fox.
23. **Babesiosis (tick-borne).** Causes anemia and liver changes; rehab success judged on clinical stabilization, with minimal time in captivity — releasable once stable.
24. **Leptospirosis.** Zoonotic bacterial disease (jaundice, kidney/liver signs); requires antibiotics, PPE, biosecurity. Guarded prognosis.
25. **Bacterial abscess/wound infection.** Bite wounds and netting abrasions frequently go septic; weeks of antibiotics. Clean healing wounds release; deep or joint infections may be fatal.
26. **Pneumonia/respiratory infection.** Common sequel of mange, distemper, or orphaning; released once lungs clear. Sometimes masks an underlying deficit (e.g. hearing loss).
27. **Eye injury or blindness.** From ulcers, fight injuries, or disease. Vision recovered → release; permanently blind foxes cannot hunt and become sanctuary residents.
28. **Avian influenza (H5N1).** Kits especially can contract it from infected birds; tremors, seizures, no approved wildlife treatment. Euthanasia recommended.

## 3. Orphaned or displaced kits

29. **Den destroyed by construction.** Builders collapse a den; cubs rescued from rubble and raised to release. Classic springtime intake.
30. **Mother killed in a road accident.** Among the most common orphan scenarios; cubs found thin and dehydrated near roads, raised in groups, released in autumn.
31. **Mother killed by hunting/trapping.** Vixens shot or snared in legal predator control leave dependent cubs; raised and released.
32. **Kits "rescued" by well-meaning public.** Walkers mistake cubs for abandoned puppies; the mother is often just moving the litter. Unnecessary rescues create orphans — and hand-reared singletons may become too tame to release.
33. **Den flooded.** Heavy rain drowns or displaces litters; flood events measurably spike intakes. Cubs arrive hypothermic, need warming and feeding.
34. **Separated while the vixen moves earths.** Vixens routinely move cubs between dens; "abandoned" cubs are often mid-move. Guidance: leave them a couple of hours — mother usually returns at night.
35. **Neonatal kit found alone.** Hours-old kits raised with fox masks, rubber gloves, and stuffed surrogates to prevent imprinting; the goal is always placement with same-age kits and release.
36. **Cub trapped in a compost bin (flystrike).** Cubs fall into garden hazards, arrive caked in rot and covered in flystrike; need repeated baths and hospital care.
37. **Cub fallen into a skip/bin.** Cubs tumble into skips and scrap bins; uninjured ones are assessed and released immediately. Sheds, bins, and riverbanks are recurring young-fox traps.
38. **Orphaned after the vixen was poisoned.** Nursing vixen dies of rodenticide; cubs starve at the earth, found emaciated. Raised like other orphans; released if they stay wild.

## 4. Human–wildlife conflict

39. **Fox trapped in a shed/garage.** Foxes wander into outbuildings and can't find the way out; healthy ones are simply freed on site.
40. **Fox down a chimney.** Soot-covered foxes in fireplaces are an occasional urban callout; uninjured animals extracted and released, burned paws or smoke inhalation mean a rehab stay.
41. **Nuisance complaint, live-trap request.** Homeowners ask for denning/bin-raiding foxes to be "taken to the countryside" — but agencies don't authorize relocation, and removed foxes are rapidly replaced. Centers answer with deterrence advice, not trapping.
42. **Head stuck in a jar or container.** Scavenging foxes wedge heads in discarded containers; quick finds are freed on the spot, prolonged cases arrive dehydrated.
43. **Bycatch in a humane trap.** Cage traps set for raccoons or cats regularly catch non-target foxes; uninjured bycatch released on site, trap injuries (broken teeth, nose abrasions) need treatment.

## 5. Captivity-related (almost always permanent sanctuary)

44. **Fur farm closure — mass rescue.** Hundreds of foxes need placement when farms close; bred for generations in cages, often in novelty color morphs. Can never be released — lifelong sanctuary residents.
45. **Fur farm culls and rejects.** Foxes surrendered as orphaned, imperfect-coated, sick, or injured — "of no value to a fur farmer." All remain in lifetime care.
46. **Surrendered pet fox.** Owners buy kits as exotic pets, surrender them when the adult fox proves destructive and difficult. Captive-bred foxes cannot legally or ethically be released.
47. **Seized illegally kept fox.** Foxes kept without permits, seized by authorities; arrive stressed and pacing. Permanent residents.
48. **Roadside zoo/menagerie closure.** Unaccredited exhibitors shut down; foxes need emergency placement. Habituated, often inbred — lifelong residents.

## 6. Habituation and imprinting

49. **Hand-reared by a finder — too tame to release.** A fox raised from a "mystery puppy" can never fend for itself; preserves have built permanent habitats for such animals, sometimes as education animals.
50. **Habituated neighborhood beggar.** Foxes raised on handouts lose fear of people and may turn aggressive over food; hard habituation is a bar to release.
51. **Escaped captive-bred fox recaptured.** Farm color morphs (silver, pearl) that escape or are dumped cannot survive or legally remain wild; taken into sanctuaries.
52. **Malimprinted singleton kit.** A kit raised alone without fox company during the critical window imprints on humans; generally deemed non-releasable. (Prevention: conspecifics, minimal human contact.)

## 7. Environmental displacement

53. **Wildfire — burns and smoke inhalation.** Burns, dehydration, smoke inhalation, starvation; treated with grafts and supportive care. Recovered animals released.
54. **Wildfire — orphaned kits.** Fires destroy earths and separate litters; very young kits may grow up in sanctuary care if too young to learn wild skills.
55. **Flood/storm displacement.** Storms destroy dens and scatter litters; big weather events measurably spike intakes. Arrivals are hypothermic, waterlogged, sometimes debris-injured.
56. **Severe winter — starvation/emaciation.** Foxes found skeletal in late winter, often with concurrent mange; recoverable ones released after regaining condition, many euthanized.

## 8. Poisoning

57. **Anticoagulant rodenticide.** Foxes eat poisoned rodents; the toxin blocks clotting, causing internal bleeding. Treated with vitamin K; guarded prognosis, hope of release.
58. **Lead poisoning.** From scavenging hunter-shot carcasses; weakness, incoordination, loss of fear. Chelation therapy, then release if recovered.
59. **Pesticide poisoning.** Tremors, salivation, seizures from sprayed fields or poisoned prey; decontamination and supportive care, severe cases die.
60. **Antifreeze ingestion.** Sweet-tasting puddles attract foxes; acute kidney failure, usually fatal by the time signs appear. Rare admissions, almost always euthanized.

## Using these well

- **The two-death rule.** The finished game contains exactly two deaths, and neither happens at the player's facility. Many seeds below would realistically end in euthanasia (distemper, rabies-suspicion, gunshot, antifreeze, catastrophic fractures) — in Fox & Found those become *referral/recognition* cases: the correct play is recognizing the case is beyond the rescue and sending it to a qualified partner, where the outcome unfolds off-screen and is reported back with care. The deaths are authored story beats, never random outcomes and never the player's fault.
- **Vary the outcomes.** Within that rule, the roster still mixes releases, sanctuary placements, referrals, and transfers.
- **Mind the welfare razor.** If a seed's natural game version would reward the player for a poor welfare choice, redesign the mechanic — don't just narrate around it.
- **Seeds are not stories.** Two cases can share a seed category (e.g. two mange cases) if their lessons differ — FOX-003 (demodectic) and FOX-004 (sarcoptic referral) already prove this.
- **Caption photos honestly.** Every image needs its licensed source, attribution, and the fiction notice per `docs/CASE-TEMPLATE.md`.
